Turning a personal problem into a policy ask

Plenty of laws started with one person's bad experience. Here is how to tell whether your problem has a government fix, and how to ask for it.

Is it a personal problem or a government one?

Ask yourself three questions:

  • Is it happening to other people too? If neighbors, coworkers or people online describe the same thing, it may be a pattern, not bad luck.
  • Does a rule cause it, or fail to prevent it? A fee, a deadline, a missing service, a law that doesn't fit how people live.
  • Is a government body responsible for that rule? If yes, you have someone to ask.

Find the right level

  • Start with the agency that runs the program. Many problems are about how a rule is carried out, not the rule itself, and the agency can fix them.
  • If the rule itself is the problem, find who wrote it: a city council, county commissioners, a district board, the Legislature, a state agency's rules, or Congress. See how to contact the people who represent you.
  • Agency rules get a second look. In Idaho, state agency rules have to go before the Legislature, which can reject them. If your problem is a rule, the agency's rulemaking comment period and the legislative committee that reviews it are both places to speak up.

Shape the ask

  1. Describe the problem in one sentence.
  2. Show it isn't just you. Collect a few other stories, and any numbers you can find.
  3. Propose a specific change. "Let people renew online," "extend the deadline to 30 days," "add a waiver for people who can't pay." You don't need perfect legal language.
  4. Find allies. Nonprofits, business groups, churches or neighborhood associations may already be working on it.
  5. Find a champion. One elected official who will carry the idea is worth more than ten who agree in passing. In the Legislature, a bill needs a sponsor and a committee chair willing to give it a hearing.

Mind the calendar

Idaho's Legislature meets for about three months starting in January, and bills that don't pass die when it adjourns. The time to bring a legislator an idea is summer and fall, when they're home and drafting. By February it is usually too late for that year.

Be ready for a long road

Most changes take more than one try. Bills often take several sessions to pass, and local changes may wait for the next budget. Keep a record of each conversation, keep your allies informed, and come back.
