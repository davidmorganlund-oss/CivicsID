Working with federal agencies

When a federal agency is stuck, slow or about to make a decision that affects you, there are ways to get through. In Idaho, where the federal government manages most of the land, that comes up often.

When you're stuck with an agency

Ask your member of Congress for help. Every House and Senate office has caseworkers who help constituents with federal agencies, including Social Security, Medicare, veterans' benefits, the IRS, passports and immigration cases.

  • Call or fill out the office's online form. Find your members with Who represents you. Each has offices around Idaho.
  • Expect to sign a privacy release, which lets the agency share your case with the office.
  • Bring case numbers and copies of letters.

Public land decisions

The Forest Service and the Bureau of Land Management manage most of Idaho's land. Grazing permits, timber sales, travel plans, mining and fire projects all go through public review.

  • Each forest and BLM district posts its projects and takes comments, usually for 30 days or more.
  • Get on the mailing list for the forest or district near you, so notices reach you while comments are open.
  • Comment early and specifically. On many projects, only people who commented can object later.
  • Local advisory groups, such as BLM resource advisory councils, have seats for residents.

Comment on a proposed rule

Federal agencies write regulations that carry out laws. Before most rules are final, the agency publishes a proposal and takes public comments.

  • Find proposed rules in the Federal Register.
  • Comment on Regulations.gov before the deadline.
  • Be specific. Agencies have to consider the substance of comments, not count them. Explain how the rule would affect you, and suggest a change.

Request records

The Freedom of Information Act lets anyone request records from federal agencies. FOIA.gov explains how and links to each agency's request page. Some records are exempt, like classified information and private personal details.

Report a problem

  • Most agencies have an Inspector General who investigates fraud, waste and abuse, and takes tips from the public.
  • Many agencies also have an ombudsman who helps resolve complaints.
