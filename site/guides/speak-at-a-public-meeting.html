How to speak at a local public meeting

City councils, county commissioners, school boards and highway districts make most of the decisions you feel day to day. Here is what Idaho law gives you, what it doesn't, and how to use two minutes well.

What Idaho law gives you

Idaho's Open Meetings Law (Idaho Code Title 74, Chapter 2) covers the governing bodies of counties, cities, school districts, special districts and state boards and commissions.

  • You can attend. Meetings of a governing body are open to the public, and decisions must be made in the open (Idaho Code 74-203).
  • You get notice. A regular meeting needs at least five calendar days' notice and an agenda posted at least 48 hours ahead. A special meeting needs 24 hours' notice of both. A board that meets on a set schedule can post that schedule once a year, but each meeting still needs its 48-hour agenda (74-204).
  • The agenda has to be real. A board can generally decide only what is on the agenda, and items it will vote on must be marked as action items. Adding something late takes a vote and a stated reason it couldn't have been listed sooner.
  • Closed sessions are limited. A board can meet privately only for reasons the law lists, such as hiring, a specific employee's discipline, buying land or pending lawsuits. It takes a two-thirds roll call vote that names the reason, and no final decision can be made behind closed doors (74-206).
  • There are minutes. Written minutes must record who was there, every motion and every vote, and be available to the public (74-205).

What it doesn't give you

The Open Meetings Law gives you the right to watch, not the right to speak. A board does not have to take public comment at an ordinary meeting. Most city councils and school boards choose to, under their own rules. Many county commissions take it less often.

Public hearings are different. When a law requires a hearing, the board must let people testify. That includes the yearly budget, most zoning and land use decisions, and many fee increases. If you want to be sure of being heard, a public hearing is the place.

Before the meeting

  1. Find the meeting. The calendar lists what's coming up near you.
  2. Read the agenda. Find the item you care about and note its number or title, and whether it is an action item or a public hearing.
  3. Check how this body takes comment. Every body sets its own rules: whether you sign up ahead or at the door, how long you get, and whether you can speak by phone or video. Call the clerk if the website doesn't say.
  4. If there's no comment period, email the members before the meeting, or ask the clerk how to get an item on a future agenda.

What to say

Time limits are short, often two or three minutes, so write it down and time yourself.

  1. Who you are. Your name and that you live, work or own a business in the community. At a hearing you'll usually be asked for your address for the record.
  2. What you're talking about. Name the agenda item.
  3. What you want them to do. Vote yes, vote no, change something specific, or delay.
  4. One reason. A short personal example lands better than a list of arguments.
  5. Repeat the ask and thank them.

After you speak

  • Don't expect an answer on the spot. Most bodies listen during comment and don't respond.
  • Follow up in writing. Email the members the same points, plus anything you ran out of time for.
  • Watch for the vote. The decision may come at a later meeting.

If a board breaks the rules

A decision made at a meeting that broke the Open Meetings Law can be thrown out. Tell the board in writing first: it then has 14 days to respond and can fix the problem by redoing the decision in the open. Complaints about local boards go to the county prosecuting attorney, and complaints about state boards go to the Attorney General. A person affected by the decision can also go to court, but only within 30 days.
