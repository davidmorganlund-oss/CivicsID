What you can do in your neighborhood

You don't need a title to change things on your block. Here are practical ways to get problems fixed and get people organized.

Get a problem fixed

  • Report it to the right place. Potholes, broken streetlights and missing signs go to whoever owns the road: the city, the county road department or the local highway district. In Ada County that is almost always the Ada County Highway District. Illegal dumping, weeds and junk vehicles go to city or county code enforcement.
  • Keep the reference number. If nothing happens, follow up with it. If nothing happens after that, contact your council member, county commissioner or highway district commissioner.
  • Bring neighbors. Five households reporting the same problem carries more weight than one.

Find out what's planned

  • Watch for land use notices. Rezones, subdivisions and special use permits require a public hearing. Notice is published, mailed to nearby property owners and usually posted on the property about two weeks ahead. If you get one, the hearing date on it is your chance to testify.
  • Ask for records. Under Idaho's Public Records Act, anyone can ask a state or local agency for its records. The agency must grant or deny the request within three working days, or tell you it needs up to ten. The first two hours of staff time and the first 100 pages are free.
  • Read the comprehensive plan. Every city and county has one, with a map of what kind of growth is planned where. Zoning decisions are supposed to follow it, so it is the best argument you can bring to a hearing.
  • Check the road plans. Highway districts and the Idaho Transportation Department publish multi-year project lists. Getting a project onto the list is easier than adding it later.

Get organized

  • Join or start a neighborhood association. Some cities formally register neighborhood associations and notify them of nearby projects.
  • Hold a block party or cleanup. Cities often have simple permits for closing a residential street, and some lend cleanup supplies.
  • Make a phone tree or group chat. It sounds small, but it is what lets a neighborhood show up quickly when a decision is coming.

Take it to a meeting

When a problem needs a decision, not just a repair, bring it to your city council, county commissioners or highway district. See how to speak at a local public meeting and turning a personal problem into a policy ask.
